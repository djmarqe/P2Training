# Episode 2: The counter. Customers, catalogs, inventory, and the VIN

Z: This is the P2 Counter, episode two. I'm Zira, Dave's with me, and today we're covering the store side of the test. Customer relations, vehicle identification, catalog skills, inventory, and merchandising.

D: Together that's twenty-five of the seventy-five scored questions, a third of the test. And here's the good news. Most of it is rules you already follow if you've worked a counter. You just need to know the rules the way ASE states them.

Z: Start with customer relations. Eleven questions. What's the pattern?

D: The pattern is that the right answer is always the one where you listen first and act second. Irate customer voicing a complaint? The answer is listen to learn how they'd like the problem resolved. Not calm down, not go get the manager, not explain what you can do. Listen first. Every time.

Z: What about the phone versus the walk-in?

D: ASE's own published sample question. You're on the phone and a customer walks in. You acknowledge the walk-in with eye contact or a quick word so they know they've been seen, then you finish the call. You don't drop the phone customer, you don't ignore the one in front of you. Acknowledge and finish.

Z: Price objections.

D: Explain the features and benefits. The customer may not know what they're paying for. And the hard rule we covered last time, never tell a customer that any product your store sells is unsafe. Not the economy pad, not the cheaper battery, nothing. If you stock it, you stand behind it.

Z: What about when the catalog doesn't have the part?

D: A customer needs an oil filter that's not listed by any aftermarket manufacturer. One specialist says check a spec manual and sell something close enough to fit. The other says offer to find the part and give the customer an estimate. Close enough is never the answer. You find the part, you quote it. The next step when the catalog can't resolve it is the dealer, the manufacturer's tech line, or your supplier's product support. Not a guess.

Z: Related sales. How does the test handle those?

D: It loves them. Almost every systems question has a related-sale angle, and we'll go through them system by system in episodes three, four, and five. The customer relations version is simpler. Suggest the related item every time, explain why it goes with the job, and never pressure. Timing belt, suggest the water pump. Brake pads, suggest the hardware kit. Fuel pump, suggest the filter.

Z: Returns.

D: A customer brings back an installed product because they were unhappy with how it performed. One specialist says record it as a lost sale. The other says put it back on the shelf as new. Neither. It's not a lost sale, you made the sale. And an installed part never goes back on the shelf as new. It gets handled as a return through whatever your store's policy is, usually a warranty claim or a defective return to the vendor.

Z: Core charges come up too.

D: A customer hesitates over a big core charge. One specialist says they can bring the core in at the time of purchase to avoid the charge. The other says the charge should be waived whenever the customer doesn't have a core. The first is right. The second is wrong. The core charge exists because the vendor needs that part back. You don't waive it, you explain it.

Z: Electronic parts.

D: Modules, sensors, computers. Typically non-returnable once installed, and the test wants you to say so at the point of sale, before the customer leaves. That's a customer relations answer and an electrical answer at the same time.

Z: Let's move to vehicle identification. Only three questions, but they're easy points.

D: The VIN is seventeen characters. Positions one through three are the manufacturer. Four through eight describe the vehicle, body, engine, restraints, series. Position nine is the check digit. Position ten is the model year. Position eleven is the assembly plant. Twelve through seventeen are the serial number.

Z: Which positions does the test actually ask about?

D: Ten, the model year, and eight, which is usually the engine code. When a catalog lists two engines for the same model, the eighth character breaks the tie. For year codes, the letters I, O, Q, U, and Z are never used, and neither is the number zero. Twenty ten is A and the letters run forward from there. Twenty twenty is L, twenty twenty-five is S, twenty twenty-six is T.

Z: Where is the VIN?

D: Visible through the windshield on the driver's side, and on the driver's door jamb label. Here's the one that catches people. Specialist A says the dashboard VIN plate tells you the country of origin. True, that's the first character. Specialist B says the production date is printed on the dashboard VIN plate. False. The production date is on the door jamb certification label, along with the gross vehicle weight rating and the tire specs. A only.

Z: Why does the production date matter?

D: Because catalogs split parts by build date. Before March, after March. If you don't know the build date, you're guessing between two part numbers. Same with paint codes and option codes, which live on the service parts label, usually in the glove box, on the trunk lid, or on the door jamb depending on the maker. GM calls their option codes RPO codes.

Z: Body styles.

D: Know your cabs. A regular cab has two doors and no rear seat. An extended cab, sometimes called a club cab, has a short rear section with small rear doors or no rear doors. A crew cab has four full-size doors and a full rear seat. The test shows you four drawings and asks which is the crew cab. It's the one with four real doors. Don't pick the one with the little half door.

Z: Catalog skills. Six questions.

D: Start every lookup with year, make, model, engine, and sub-model. Then narrow with build date, VIN, drivetrain, and options. And then read the footnotes. I cannot say this strongly enough. The footnotes are where it says before production date, with heavy duty cooling, four wheel drive only. Skipping the footnote is the single most common reason a wrong part goes out the door.

Z: Application versus interchange.

D: An application lookup finds the part for a vehicle. You have the car, you want the part. An interchange, or cross-reference, finds an equivalent part number from another brand or from the OE number. You have a number, you want the matching number. So if a customer hands you an EGR valve with an OEM part number stamped on it and wants the aftermarket equivalent, the best source is the cross-reference guide. Not the spec guide, not the illustrated guide, not the buyer's guide.

Z: Alphanumeric sorting.

D: The test gives you four part numbers and asks which order they'd sit on a shelf, left to right. The rule is character by character. A two seven six zero comes before A two seven six zero A, because the shorter one ends first. Then A two seven six zero A X. Then anything starting with B. Letters after numbers at the same position, shorter before longer.

Z: Reading catalog tables.

D: This one's a reading-speed trap. A hub table has columns for hub part number, hub assembly number, bolt pattern, pilot diameter, inner bearing, outer bearing, and capacity. The question asks for the inner bearing for a six bolt hub rated at thirty-five sixty pounds. You find the row, that's easy. Then you have to read across to the inner bearing column, not stop at the first column. The hub part number is the trap answer. Slow down on tables.

Z: Web catalogs.

D: Specialist A says the latest catalog information is on the vendor's web catalog. True, a website updates instantly while printed supplements and discs take weeks. Specialist B says a special login and password may be needed to access it. Also true, lots of vendor catalogs are restricted to authorized sellers. Both.

Z: Anything else on catalogs?

D: Supplements and technical bulletins update a printed catalog between editions, so a printed catalog is only as current as its latest supplement. Keeping the electronic catalog updated and reporting a catalog error back to the supplier are both listed job tasks. And use the illustration to confirm the shape and the connector before you commit.

Z: Inventory management. Three questions, and they're all definitions.

D: Inventory turnover is cost of goods sold divided by average inventory value. Higher turns mean your dollars are working harder. Very low turns mean dead stock sitting on a shelf.

Z: Lost sales.

D: Must be recorded. Every time. A lost sale is when a customer asked for something and you didn't have it. Logging it is how the system learns what to stock. If you don't log it, the store keeps missing the same sale forever. And remember what's not a lost sale. A return isn't. A sale you made and then refunded isn't.

Z: Physical inventory.

D: Counting what's actually on the shelf and comparing it to what the computer says. It exposes shrinkage, misplaced stock, and receiving errors. Discrepancies get reported, not quietly adjusted.

Z: There's a math question in here somewhere.

D: There is. The computer shows sixteen cans. You invoice one can. When the order is pulled, somebody grabs a whole case of twelve by mistake. What does the computer show, and what's on the shelf? The computer only knows about the one you invoiced, so it says fifteen. The shelf actually lost twelve, so there are four. Fifteen in the system, four on the shelf. The computer never finds out about the mistake until someone counts.

Z: Stock rotation.

D: First in, first out. New stock goes behind existing stock. It matters most for anything with a shelf life. Chemicals, paint, belts, hoses, and batteries. Specialist A says FIFO keeps battery stock fresh. True. Specialist B says checking a battery's voltage at the time of sale will reduce returns. Also true, because you catch the discharged one before it leaves. Both.

Z: Cores.

D: Inspect before you accept. Same part, complete, not cracked, not otherwise unrebuildable. Tag it and store it to match the invoice. A core that doesn't match the part sold doesn't get credited.

Z: Special orders and store-use.

D: Special orders normally need customer information, sometimes a deposit, and may carry a handling or restocking charge if the customer abandons it. Explain that up front so there's no argument later. Store-use items, the rag or the brake cleaner the shop grabs off the shelf, still have to be written off the inventory. Taking it off the shelf without writing it off is how the count goes wrong.

Z: And the purchase order one more time.

D: Buyer's written authorization to purchase. From the customer to you, or from you to your supplier. It is not a shipping confirmation.

Z: Last area, merchandising. Two questions.

D: Displays sell what customers didn't come in for. Impulse items go at the counter and near the register. Seasonal items go on end caps and in high-traffic aisles ahead of the season. Wipers and antifreeze in fall, A/C products and coolant in spring. Related items get cross-merchandised next to their primary part, brake cleaner next to the brake pads.

Z: Shelf rules.

D: Everything displayed is clearly priced. Shelves are faced and fronted, meaning product pulled forward and lined up, and kept full. A gap on a shelf reads as out of stock even when you have ten in the back. Eye level is the premium position. Planograms tell you where things go. And damaged or faded packaging gets pulled, because it reads as old stock and hurts the whole display.

Z: Quick-fire review. Irate customer.

D: Listen first.

Z: Cheaper product safety.

D: Never say any product you sell is unsafe.

Z: VIN position ten.

D: Model year.

Z: Production date location.

D: Door jamb label, not the dash plate.

Z: Crew cab.

D: Four full doors.

Z: OEM number to aftermarket number.

D: Cross-reference guide.

Z: Inventory turnover.

D: Cost of goods sold over average inventory.

Z: Lost sales.

D: Record every one.

Z: Stock rotation.

D: First in, first out. New behind old.

Z: Purchase order.

D: Buyer's authorization to purchase.

D: Bring a notebook for that one.
