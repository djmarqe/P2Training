# Episode 5: Wires, cold air, batteries, and the parts nobody studies

Z: P2 Counter, episode five, the last one. I'm Zira, Dave's here. Today we finish vehicle systems with HVAC, electrical, battery and charging, and the miscellaneous category. Then we'll close with how to spend your final week.

D: Twelve scored questions in this episode. HVAC three, electrical three, battery charging and starting three, and miscellaneous three. And I want to say this up front. Miscellaneous is fasteners, fittings, hose, belts, and body supplies. It's worth exactly as many questions as brakes. Most people never open that section. Open it.

Z: HVAC first.

D: Compressor and clutch, condenser, evaporator, expansion device, and a drier. The thing the test loves is that there are two system designs and they use different parts. An orifice tube system uses an accumulator, which sits on the low side after the evaporator. A thermal expansion valve system, TXV, uses a receiver-drier, which sits on the high side. Orifice tube, accumulator. Expansion valve, receiver-drier. That pairing is a guaranteed catalog question.

Z: Refrigerants.

D: Three. R-12 is obsolete. R-134a is most of what's on the road. R-1234yf is on newer vehicles. They are not interchangeable and they use different fittings so you can't accidentally mix them. And restrictions on who can buy certain refrigerants come from the EPA. Not OSHA, not DOT, and not the SDS, which is a document, not an agency.

Z: Compressor oil.

D: Has to match the system. PAG oil in most R-134a systems. Mineral oil in R-12. And POE oil in hybrid and electric vehicle compressors, because PAG conducts electricity and can cause a high-voltage fault in an electric compressor. If a question mentions a hybrid compressor, the answer involves POE.

Z: Related sales on a compressor.

D: This one's required, not optional. New accumulator or receiver-drier, new orifice tube or expansion valve, and a system flush. The drier gets replaced any time the system is opened because the desiccant inside saturates with moisture. Then O-rings, refrigerant oil, and a cabin air filter round out the ticket.

Z: Electrical. Three questions.

D: Fuses, fusible links, circuit breakers, relays, switches, connectors, grounds, and modules. Fuse types, ATO and ATC blade, mini, low-profile mini, maxi, and cartridge. Two rules. A fuse protects the wiring, not the device, so never put in a higher amp fuse than specified. And a fuse element is designed to melt when a current-carrying wire is shorted to ground. Not when there's a break in the wiring, a break stops current. Not when resistance is too high, high resistance means less current. A short to ground, that's the overload that blows a fuse.

Z: Wire.

D: Wire gauge runs backwards. A lower AWG number is a bigger wire. Ten gauge is much heavier than eighteen gauge. Longer runs and higher current need heavier wire, so a lower number.

Z: Bulbs.

D: Trade numbers. An eleven fifty-seven is dual filament, park and brake. An eleven fifty-six is single filament. A one ninety-four is a small wedge bulb. Composite headlamps use a replaceable capsule like a ninety-oh-six or an H-eleven. A sealed beam is one piece. Halogen capsules must not be touched with bare fingers, because the oil from your skin creates a hot spot that fails the bulb.

Z: And the point-of-sale rule.

D: Electronic modules and sensors are typically non-returnable once installed. Tell the customer before they pay. That's listed as a task and it shows up as a question.

Z: Battery, charging, and starting. Three questions, and the ratings are definitions you just have to know.

D: CCA, cold cranking amps, is the amps a battery delivers for thirty seconds at zero degrees Fahrenheit while holding at least seven point two volts. That's the number that matters in a cold climate. CA or MCA is the same test at thirty-two degrees, so it's always a bigger, less meaningful number. Reserve capacity is the minutes a battery can carry a twenty-five amp load before dropping to ten and a half volts.

Z: Group size.

D: Group size is physical. Case dimensions, terminal type, and terminal location. Never substitute a group that doesn't fit the tray or that reverses the posts, because the cables won't reach or they'll touch the wrong things. And never sell a battery with less CCA than the OE spec.

Z: Voltage numbers.

D: A fully charged twelve-volt battery reads about twelve point six open circuit. Twelve point four is about seventy-five percent. Twelve point two is fifty. Twelve point oh is twenty-five. Specific gravity at full charge is about one point two six five. A load test applies half the CCA rating for fifteen seconds, and the battery must stay above nine point six volts at seventy degrees. And you charge a battery before you load test it. Testing a discharged battery just tells you it's discharged.

Z: AGM.

D: AGM batteries need an AGM-compatible charger. They're standard on most start-stop vehicles, and an AGM-equipped car should get an AGM replacement. Don't downgrade.

Z: The two Specialist A and B questions from this section.

D: First one. A missing battery hold-down could damage the battery. True, vibration kills batteries. A faulty voltage regulator could cause a battery to fail. Also true, overcharging boils it, undercharging sulfates it. Both. Second one we covered in episode two. FIFO keeps battery stock fresh, and checking voltage at the sale cuts returns. Both.

Z: Charging and starting.

D: Charging output is typically thirteen and a half to fourteen and a half volts at idle. Alternators are specified by amp output, pulley type, and mounting. Clutch pulleys, called OAD or decoupler pulleys, are common now. Starters are specified by rotation, tooth count, and mounting. Both are common reman core items. Related sales, terminals, cables, protector washers, terminal cleaner, and a hold-down. On hybrids and EVs, the high-voltage system is in orange cable and is not a DIY item.

Z: Now the miscellaneous category. Fasteners first.

D: SAE bolt grades are read by radial lines on the head. No marks is grade two. Three lines is grade five. Six lines is grade eight. More lines, more strength. Metric bolts are stamped with a number instead, eight point eight, ten point nine, twelve point nine, higher is stronger. Never substitute a lower grade into a critical application.

Z: Fastener callouts.

D: SAE is diameter, threads per inch, length. Three-eighths sixteen by one is three-eighths inch diameter, sixteen threads per inch, which is coarse, one inch long. Fine thread in three-eighths is twenty-four. Metric is diameter, pitch, length. M ten by one point five by thirty is ten millimeters diameter with one and a half millimeters between threads. Here's the trap. Metric pitch is the distance between threads, so a smaller number is finer. SAE is threads per inch, so a bigger number is finer. Opposite conventions. And SAE and metric are never interchangeable even if they thread in a turn or two.

Z: Fittings.

D: NPT is tapered pipe thread and seals on the threads, so it needs sealant. Inverted flare, double flare, and ISO bubble flare are for brake and fuel lines and seal on the flare, not the threads. AN or JIC is a thirty-seven degree flare. Compression fittings are low pressure only. Never on brakes.

Z: Hose.

D: Hose is sized by inside diameter. If a drawing shows a hose with a three-quarter inch outside and one-eighth inch walls, the hose is half inch. The outside number is the trap. And hose is matched to the fluid. Heater hose for coolant. Fuel hose for fuel. Vacuum hose for vacuum. PCV hose for PCV. Transmission cooler hose for ATF. Power steering hose for power steering fluid. They are not cross-substitutable, and the question is always written so the cheap substitute is the wrong answer.

Z: Belts.

D: A V-belt is measured by effective outside length and rides on the pulley's side walls. A serpentine belt is specified by rib count and length. Six K eight eighty-five is six ribs, eighty-eight and a half inches. A worn serpentine belt is judged by rib material loss with a wear gauge now, not by cracks. Related sales, the tensioner, the idler pulley, and any belt-driven accessory that's making noise.

Z: Body and refinish.

D: Primer, sealer, base coat, clear coat, reducer, hardener, body filler, glazing putty, sandpaper grits, masking, and respirators. Paint chemicals have a shelf life, so new stock goes behind old. VOC rules limit what can be sold in some regions.

Z: Alright. Final week. What do I actually do?

Two, take the official ASE practice test at asepractice dot com so the interface holds no surprises, and do the free demo so you've seen the buttons. Three, drill the math by hand. Margin, markup, stacked discounts, cost per month. No calculator in the room. Four, memorize the short lists. Fastener grade lines, DOT fluid ratings, accumulator versus receiver-drier, battery ratings, VIN positions. High frequency, low effort. Five, stop changing answers.
