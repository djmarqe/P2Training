# Episode 3: Under the hood. Engine, cooling, fuel, ignition, exhaust, emissions

Z: P2 Counter, episode three. I'm Zira, Dave's here, and we're starting vehicle systems. This is forty of the seventy-five scored questions, so we're splitting it across three episodes.

D: And inside those forty, ASE publishes the split. Engine mechanical three questions, cooling two, fuel three, ignition three, exhaust two, emissions three. That's sixteen questions in this episode alone. More than the whole customer relations section.

Z: Engine mechanical. What do I need?

D: Know the major parts. Block, crankshaft, connecting rods, pistons and rings, camshaft, cylinder head, valves, timing set, oil pump, and gaskets. Then know the handful of facts the test keeps coming back to.

Z: Start with timing belts.

D: A timing belt is a scheduled maintenance item, typically somewhere between sixty and a hundred and five thousand miles. On an interference engine, if the belt breaks the valves hit the pistons and you have major damage. A timing chain is generally a lifetime part but it stretches, and when it's replaced the guides and tensioner go with it. Related sales on a timing belt job are the water pump, the tensioner, the idler pulleys, and coolant. The water pump is the big one, because it's behind the belt and the labor is already paid for.

Z: Gaskets.

D: Head gasket failure shows as coolant loss, white exhaust smoke, milky oil, or overheating. A rear main seal leaks at the back of the block between the engine and transmission. And the gasket set question. Specialist A says a complete gasket set may be the best value for a customer doing an overhaul. Specialist B says some gaskets are only available in a complete set. Both true. And one more. Those semi-circular rubber plugs that seal the ends of the camshaft on an overhead cam engine? They come in the valve cover gasket set.

Z: Bearings and oil.

D: Main bearings support the crankshaft. Rod bearings ride on the rod journals. We covered the undersize decimals in episode one. Core plugs, which everybody calls freeze plugs, seal the casting holes in the block. The harmonic balancer damps crankshaft vibration. Oil is specified by SAE viscosity grade plus the API service rating, and the current gasoline rating is API SP. Related sales on an oil change, filter, drain plug gasket, and RTV if the pan's coming off.

Z: Cooling. Two questions.

D: Radiator, pressure cap, thermostat, water pump, hoses and clamps, fan, overflow tank, heater core. Two parts do the thinking. The thermostat controls engine operating temperature, and it's rated by its opening temperature, commonly one eighty or one ninety-five degrees. An engine that never warms up usually has a thermostat stuck open. The pressure cap raises the boiling point, roughly three degrees per pound of pressure. The wrong cap means overheating or a collapsed hose.

Z: Coolant types.

D: Three families. Conventional green, called IAT, about two years or thirty thousand miles. Orange OAT, like Dex-Cool, five years or a hundred fifty thousand. And yellow or gold HOAT. Don't mix them, and match the manufacturer's spec, not just the color. Mix concentrate fifty-fifty with distilled water. Never tap water.

Z: Related sales on a radiator.

D: Hoses, clamps, the cap, the thermostat and its gasket, and coolant. Now, the except version of this question. Related sales on a radiator except which one? The answer is the evaporator. The evaporator is an air conditioning part. It's in the dash. It has nothing to do with the cooling system. It just sounds like it should.

Z: Fuel. Three questions, and I know this is where people lose points.

D: The parts. Fuel tank, in-tank electric pump and sending unit, filter, lines, rail, injectors, and a pressure regulator. Modern returnless systems regulate pressure at the tank. Older return systems have a regulator on the rail with a vacuum line. Direct injection, GDI, runs very high rail pressure and uses a mechanical high-pressure pump driven off the camshaft on top of the in-tank pump.

Z: The injector seal.

D: O-rings. A port fuel injector seals with an O-ring at the rail end and an O-ring at the manifold end. Not a gasket, not sealant, not a retaining clip. The clip holds it in place, the O-ring seals it. Replace the O-rings any time an injector comes out.

Z: The fuel pump related sale.

D: The fuel filter. Here's why. A clogged filter starves the pump and that's what killed the old one. And the debris that killed it is still in the system, so a new pump will push more of it downstream. Pump and filter together, every time. Also offer the tank seal and the strainer sock. It's not the vapor canister, it's not the pressure regulator, it's not injectors.

Z: Hose.

D: Fuel-rated hose is the only hose that may be used on a fuel system. Heater hose, vacuum hose, generic rubber, they'll all fail and it's a fire. And fuel injection hose is rated for high pressure and is not interchangeable with low-pressure carburetor hose. If a question tempts you with the cheaper hose, that's the wrong answer.

Z: Ignition. Three questions.

D: Three generations of ignition, and the test wants you to know what parts each one has. Old distributor systems have a cap, a rotor, plug wires, and a single coil. Distributorless, called DIS or waste spark, uses coil packs and plug wires but has no cap and no rotor. Coil on plug, COP, puts an individual coil on each spark plug and has no plug wires at all.

Z: So the trap is.

D: Somebody asks for a part that doesn't exist on their system. A customer with a DIS vehicle asks for plug wires, an ignition coil, a crank sensor, and a pick-up coil. Which one do you question? The pick-up coil. A pick-up coil lives inside a distributor. No distributor, no pick-up coil. The crank sensor does that job now. Same idea if someone wants a cap and rotor for a coil-on-plug car. The part doesn't exist for that application.

Z: Spark plugs.

D: Specified by thread diameter, reach, heat range, and electrode material. Copper, platinum, double platinum, iridium. Heat range describes how fast the plug sheds heat. A hot plug runs hotter and resists fouling. A cold plug sheds heat faster and resists pre-ignition. Gap to the vehicle spec, and don't aggressively re-gap fine-wire platinum or iridium plugs. Most modern plugs and wires are resistor type to suppress radio noise.

Z: Plug wires.

D: Here's the except question. Features of a quality plug wire except? High resistance fiber cores. A quality wire has low resistance, usually a spiral wound core, with good insulation, heat resistant boots, and corrosion resistant terminals. High resistance is the bad one. Related sales on plugs, a wire set, boot grease, anti-seize, and coils.

Z: Exhaust. Two questions.

D: Exhaust manifold, head pipe, flex pipe, catalytic converter, oxygen sensors, resonator, muffler, tailpipe, hangers, clamps, gaskets. The regulatory part is the converter. It's a federally regulated emissions part. Replacements have to meet EPA or CARB rules, CARB-compliant units are required in California and the states that follow California, and the converter carries a federal emissions warranty, generally eight years or eighty thousand miles. And never replace a converter without figuring out why it failed, because whatever killed it will kill the new one.

Z: The hanger question.

D: A customer wants every component and every rubber hanger for the system in the illustration. The diagram shows the hanger part number at several points along the system. You count the callouts on the drawing. Every place a hanger is drawn is a hanger you sell, even though they all share one part number and the parts table only lists it once. Count the picture, not the table.

Z: Emissions. Three questions.

D: Four systems. PCV, EGR, EVAP, and the oxygen sensors. PCV vents crankcase blow-by back into the intake. The valve and the fresh-air breather hose are the service parts, and PCV must use PCV-rated hose, not heater hose. Heater hose isn't oil-resistant and it will swell and fail.

Z: EGR.

D: Exhaust gas recirculation. Despite the name, the EGR valve is normally mounted on the intake manifold. Its job is to meter a small amount of exhaust into the intake charge, which lowers combustion temperature and cuts NOx. People hear exhaust and pick exhaust manifold. Intake manifold.

Z: EVAP.

D: Captures fuel tank vapors in a charcoal canister and purges them into the engine. The most common failure is a loose or bad gas cap. After that, the purge and vent solenoids. If a customer has an evap code, the gas cap is the first thing to sell.

Z: Oxygen sensors.

D: Upstream sensor, before the converter, is the fuel-trim feedback sensor. Downstream sensor, after the converter, monitors converter efficiency. Air injection systems pump air into the exhaust to help the converter.

Z: Modules and sensors.

D: Specialist A says powertrain control modules should be programmed before they're stocked on the shelf. False. They're programmed to the specific vehicle at installation. Specialist B says some throttle position sensors must be calibrated after installation. True. B only.

Z: Before the quick-fire, let's do a few the way the test would ask them. You read, I'll answer, you tell me if I'm right.

D: Here's one. A customer's engine never reaches operating temperature and the heater blows lukewarm. Which part is most likely at fault? Radiator cap, thermostat, water pump, or heater core.

Z: Thermostat stuck open.

D: Right. The thermostat controls operating temperature. If it's stuck open, coolant circulates constantly and the engine never warms up. Next. Specialist A says a radiator pressure cap raises the boiling point of the coolant. Specialist B says a cap with too low a pressure rating can cause overheating. Who's right?

Z: Both. The cap raises the boiling point about three degrees a pound, so a low-rated cap lets it boil sooner.

D: Both is correct. Here's an except question. All of these are related sales with a timing belt except. Water pump, tensioner, idler pulley, or oil pump.

Z: Oil pump. The water pump, tensioner, and idlers all live behind the belt. The oil pump doesn't.

D: Good. Now fuel. A customer with a carbureted pickup wants to replace a cracked fuel line with a length of heater hose because it's the same size. What do you tell them?

Z: No. Only fuel-rated hose goes on a fuel system. Heater hose isn't fuel resistant and it will fail.

D: Right, and for a fuel-injected car it has to be the high-pressure injection hose, not the carburetor grade. Ignition. A customer with a coil-on-plug engine asks for a set of plug wires. What's your response?

Z: That engine doesn't use plug wires. Each coil sits directly on its plug. If something's misfiring, they probably need a coil or plugs.

D: Exactly. Emissions. A check engine light with an evaporative system code. What's the first part to check and sell?

Z: The gas cap.

D: And the oxygen sensor question. Which sensor does the computer use for fuel trim, upstream or downstream?

Z: Upstream, before the converter. Downstream just watches the converter.
