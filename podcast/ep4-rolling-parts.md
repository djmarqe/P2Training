# Episode 4: Rolling parts. Clutch, automatic, driveline, brakes, suspension

Z: P2 Counter, episode four. Ava and Dave. Today it's everything from the back of the engine to the road. Manual transmission, automatic transmission, drive train, brakes, and suspension, steering, and wheels.

D: Twelve scored questions. Manual two, automatic two, drive train two, brakes three, suspension and steering three. And brakes is the one with the most rules to memorize, so we'll spend extra time there.

Z: Manual transmission and the clutch.

D: The parts. Clutch disc, pressure plate, release bearing, pilot bearing or bushing, flywheel, clutch fork, and on hydraulic systems, a master and slave cylinder. A clutch kit normally contains the disc, the pressure plate, and the release bearing. The pilot bearing and an alignment tool are the classic related sales, along with resurfacing or replacing the flywheel.

Z: The bearing question.

D: Which bearing contacts the pressure plate when you push the pedal? The release bearing, also called the throwout bearing. It rides on the input shaft collar, the fork pushes it into the pressure plate fingers, and that releases the clutch. The pilot bearing is the trap answer. It sits in the end of the crankshaft and supports the tip of the input shaft. It never touches the pressure plate. The input and output shaft bearings are inside the transmission.

Z: Gear oil.

D: Rated by API GL number, and here's the catch. GL-5 has high-sulfur extreme pressure additives that can attack the brass and bronze synchronizers in some manual transmissions. So when the manufacturer calls for GL-4, you sell GL-4. Never substitute upward without checking. More number is not automatically better.

Z: Automatic transmission.

D: Torque converter, flexplate, valve body, pump, bands and clutches, filter and pan gasket, cooler and cooler lines. The illustration question is usually the filter. It's a flat, pan-shaped part with a pickup tube and a few holes, and the wrong answers are the valve body, a mass airflow sensor, and a cabin air filter.

Z: Fluid.

D: Not universal. Dexron and Mercon families, Ford Type F, Chrysler ATF plus four, and CVT fluid are chemically different. The big one, CVT fluid and conventional ATF are not interchangeable. Wrong fluid in a CVT destroys it. The standard service sale is a filter kit with the pan gasket, plus the right fluid, and RTV where the manufacturer specifies it instead of a gasket.

Z: Drive train.

D: Driveshaft, universal joints, center support bearing, slip yoke. CV axles with inner and outer joints and boots. The differential with ring and pinion, carrier, and axle shafts. Transfer case on four wheel drive.

Z: The center support bearing question.

D: A center support bearing holds up the middle of a two-piece driveshaft. To replace it, the driveshaft comes out. Once the driveshaft is out, the U-joints get replaced at the same time. So the related sale is U-joints. Not the transmission seal, not the differential cover gasket, not a CV axle. The driveshaft is already in your hands, do the U-joints.

Z: CV axles.

D: A torn CV boot is the number one reason a CV axle gets replaced. The boot tears, the grease slings out, dirt gets in, the joint fails. Boot kits are the related sale if you catch it early. Limited-slip differentials require a friction modifier additive or they chatter in turns. U-joints are sized by cap diameter and snap-ring style. Related sales on a differential job, gear oil, RTV or a cover gasket, and axle seals.

Z: Brakes. Three questions. Let's go slow.

D: The parts. Master cylinder, vacuum booster or hydro-boost, lines and hoses, proportioning or combination valve, calipers, wheel cylinders, pads, shoes, rotors, drums, hardware and spring kits, the ABS module and wheel speed sensors.

Z: Friction material.

D: Three types. Organic, sometimes called NAO, is quiet and easy on rotors but wears faster. Semi-metallic handles heat and heavy loads but is noisier and harder on rotors. Ceramic is quiet, low-dust, and light-colored, and it's the usual premium upsell for a daily driver. Always sell pads in axle sets, both sides, never one wheel.

Z: Brake fluid. This is the memorize part.

D: DOT 3 and DOT 4 are glycol-based and hygroscopic. That means they absorb moisture from the air, and moisture lowers the boiling point. That's why the container is sealed and why you never reuse an open bottle. DOT 4 has a higher boiling point than DOT 3. Glycol fluid strips paint, so you keep it off the fender.

Z: DOT 5.

D: DOT 5 is silicone. It is not compatible with DOT 3 or 4, and it's not used in most ABS vehicles. Now the trick. DOT 5.1 is glycol-based despite the number, and it is compatible with 3 and 4. If a question wants the odd one out, it's DOT 5. Not 5.1.

Z: Lines and fittings.

D: Steel brake line uses a double flare, which is SAE, or an ISO bubble flare. Compression fittings must never be used on a brake line. If the question offers a compression fitting as a brake repair, that's the wrong answer every time.

Z: Lubricants.

D: What goes on a caliper mounting or sliding surface? Silicone brake grease, a high-temp brake lubricant. Not dielectric compound, that's for electrical connectors. Not extreme pressure gear grease. Not anti-seize on a slide pin, it'll gum up.

Z: Drum brake parts.

D: The illustration questions. The adjuster lever is the lever that ratchets the star wheel to take up lining wear as the shoes move. The parking brake lever, sometimes called the emergency cable lever, is the lever the cable pulls to push the shoes out. The equalizer is the bracket that balances cable tension between the two rear wheels. The cable guide routes the cable around the shoe. If the drawing shows a lever with a pivot and a little pawl end that would sit on a star wheel, that's the adjuster lever.

Z: Related sales on a brake job.

D: Hardware kit, caliper bracket bolts and slide pin grease, brake cleaner, fluid, rotors, and a bleeder wrench. And pads in axle sets.

Z: Suspension, steering, and wheels. Three questions.

D: Parts. Struts, which carry the spring and locate the wheel on a MacPherson design. Shocks. Coil and leaf springs. Upper and lower control arms, ball joints, bushings. Sway bar and end links. Inner and outer tie rod ends. Idler arm and pitman arm on parallelogram steering. Rack and pinion. Steering gearbox and power steering pump. Wheel bearings and hub assemblies.

Z: The illustration question.

D: They show an exploded strut suspension with letters. Which one is the lower control arm bushing? Find the lower control arm, the arm at the bottom that runs from the frame out to the knuckle. The bushings are the rubber sleeves where it bolts to the frame. Not the strut mount at the top, not the sway bar link, not the ball joint at the outer end.

Z: The alignment rule.

D: Replacing struts, or any part that changes suspension geometry, requires an alignment. That's the related sale. Along with strut mounts and bearing plates, which are included if you sell a loaded quick-strut assembly. Camber is the inward or outward tilt seen from the front. Caster is the forward or rearward tilt of the steering axis seen from the side. Toe is whether the wheels point in or out seen from above. And toe wear is the most common cause of rapid, feathered tire wear.

Z: Wheels.

D: Specified by diameter, width, bolt pattern, offset, backspacing, and center bore. Bolt pattern is lugs times bolt circle, like five by one fourteen point three. Offset is the distance from the mounting face to the wheel centerline. Positive offset pushes the wheel inboard. Lug nuts have to match the seat type, conical, ball, or flat with a washer, and they get torqued to spec in a star pattern. TPMS sensors need a service kit and usually a relearn after tire work.

Z: Let's do practice questions before the review. Same deal, you read them test-style and I'll answer.

D: Clutch first. A customer is buying a clutch kit for a manual transmission. Specialist A says the kit normally includes the disc, pressure plate, and release bearing. Specialist B says the pilot bearing is normally included in the kit. Who's right?

Z: A only. The pilot bearing is the related sale, not part of the kit. Along with the alignment tool and the flywheel.

D: Correct. Gear oil. A customer's manual transmission calls for GL-4 and you only have GL-5 on the shelf. Specialist A says GL-5 is a higher rating so it's fine. Specialist B says GL-5's additives can damage brass synchronizers. Who's right?

Z: B only. You don't substitute up on gear oil. Order the GL-4.

D: Automatic. A customer wants transmission fluid for a vehicle with a continuously variable transmission. They say any Dexron will do. What do you tell them?

Z: No. CVT fluid is specific and regular ATF will destroy a CVT. Sell the CVT fluid the manufacturer calls for.

D: Drive train. A customer says their front-wheel-drive car clicks when turning. What are they most likely going to need?

Z: A CV axle, and the reason is almost always a torn boot that let the grease out. If the boot had been caught early, a boot kit would have saved the joint.

D: Right. Limited slip. A customer refilled their limited-slip rear end and now it chatters in turns. What did they forget?

Z: The friction modifier additive.

D: Brakes. Here's the except question. All of these brake fluids are glycol-based and compatible with each other except. DOT 3, DOT 4, DOT 5, DOT 5.1.

Z: DOT 5. It's silicone. And 5.1 is the one that sounds like it should be the odd one out but isn't.

D: Perfect. A customer wants to repair a leaking steel brake line with a compression fitting from the plumbing aisle. What's your answer?

Z: Never on a brake line. It has to be a proper double flare or ISO flare union, or replace the section of line.

D: A customer is buying ceramic pads for the front of a daily driver. They ask for two pads for just the side that's worn. What do you do?

Z: Sell the axle set. Pads always go on in pairs, both sides of the same axle, or the car pulls and wears unevenly.

D: Suspension. A customer is replacing both front struts. What's the related sale you can't skip?

Z: An alignment. Anything that changes suspension geometry needs one. And strut mounts, or sell the loaded assembly that includes them.

D: One more. A customer's tires show rapid wear with a feathered edge across the tread. Which alignment angle is the usual cause?

Z: Toe.

D: You've got it. Quick-fire.

Z: Quick-fire. Bearing that contacts the pressure plate.

D: Release bearing. Throwout bearing.

Z: Pilot bearing location.

D: End of the crankshaft.

Z: GL-5 danger.

D: Attacks brass synchros. Use GL-4 where specified.

Z: CVT fluid.

D: Not interchangeable with regular ATF.

Z: Sold with a center support bearing.

D: U-joints.

Z: Number one reason for CV axle replacement.

D: Torn boot.

Z: Limited slip needs.

D: Friction modifier.

Z: DOT 5 is.

D: Silicone. Not compatible with 3 and 4.

Z: DOT 5.1 is.

D: Glycol. Compatible with 3 and 4.

Z: Compression fittings on brake lines.

D: Never.

Z: Caliper slide lube.

D: Silicone brake grease.

Z: Ratchets the star wheel.

D: Adjuster lever.

Z: Replace struts, sell.

D: An alignment.

Z: Most common cause of feathered tire wear.

D: Toe.
