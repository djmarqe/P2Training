# Episode 1: How the test works, and the math that trips people

Z: Welcome to the P2 Counter, a five-part audio study series for the ASE P2 Automobile Parts Specialist test. I'm Ava, and I'll be asking the questions.

D: And I'm Dave. I've worked a parts counter for a long time, and I've helped a lot of people get through this test. Over five episodes we're going to cover the whole blueprint, but we're going to spend our time where the points are.

Z: So let's start with the test itself. What are we walking into?

D: Eighty-five questions, ninety minutes, on a computer. Here's the first thing most people don't know. Ten of those eighty-five questions don't count. They're research questions ASE is trying out, and they're mixed in with no marking at all. So your score comes from seventy-five questions, and you have no way to tell which ten are the freebies.

Z: Which means?

D: Which means you answer every single question. There is no penalty for guessing. A blank is a guaranteed zero and a guess is a one-in-four shot at minimum. Never leave one blank.

Z: What's a passing score?

D: ASE doesn't publish a fixed number. People who've taken it treat roughly seventy percent as the realistic target. And remember, passing the test alone doesn't make you certified. You also need two years of parts experience, and training can substitute for up to one of those years. The certification is good for five years.

Z: Alright, so where are the points?

D: Seven content areas. Let me give you the big three first, because they're eighty-one percent of the test. Vehicle systems knowledge is forty questions out of seventy-five. That's fifty-three percent. More than half the test is knowing what parts do and what sells with them. Customer relations and sales skills is eleven questions, fifteen percent. General operations, which is mostly math and safety, is ten questions, thirteen percent.

Z: And the small ones?

D: Catalog and information systems, six questions. Vehicle identification, three. Inventory management, three. Merchandising, two. Those four areas together are only fourteen questions. So if you're short on study time, you over-study vehicle systems and math, and you skim merchandising the night before.

Z: Let's talk about question formats, because I know the test uses a few.

D: Five formats, and knowing them is worth real points because the traps are predictable. First is the plain direct question. Stem, four choices, one right answer. That's most of the test.

Z: Second?

D: The EXCEPT question. The stem contains the word except in capital letters, and the test actually warns you with a sentence that says this question is not like the others. Three of the four choices are true, and you're hunting for the one that's false. The trick is to read it as which of these is not. People blow these by answering on autopilot and picking the first true thing they see.

Z: Third format.

D: Parts Specialist A and Parts Specialist B. Two statements, then four options. A only, B only, both, or neither. Here's how you handle it. Judge each statement completely on its own before you even look at the options. If a statement is mostly right but has one wrong word, it's wrong. And this is important, both and neither are correct far more often than people expect. Don't avoid them.

Z: I think that's where a lot of people lose points.

D: It is. Let me give you a real example of the trap. A customer objects to the price of a premium product. Specialist A says explain the features and benefits. Specialist B says tell the customer the cheaper option may not be as safe. People pick both, because B sounds like a sales technique. But ASE's rule is absolute. You never tell a customer that anything your store sells is unsafe. So A is true, B is false, the answer is A only.

Z: Fourth format.

D: Most likely and least likely. All four answers might be technically possible. You're ranking probability, not truth. Pick the everyday, common-sense counter answer.

Z: And fifth?

D: Illustration questions. A drawing of a part, and you identify it or a feature of it. On P2 these lean on sensors, fastener head markings, fitting types, brake and suspension parts, and catalog diagrams. Sometimes they ask you to count things on a diagram, like how many rubber hangers an exhaust system needs. On those, count the callouts drawn on the picture, not the number of lines in the parts table. One part number can appear at three different mounting points.

Z: Any test-day tactics before we get into the math?

D: Three. One, read the stem twice before you look at the answers, especially anything with except, not, least, or all. Two, cover the choices and predict the answer first. If your prediction is on the list, it's usually right. Three, and this is the one that costs people the most, do not change an answer on review unless you find a concrete reason. Your first instinct on this test is usually better than your second. I've watched people change two right answers to wrong ones in a single sitting.

Z: Timing?

D: Ninety minutes over eighty-five questions is about sixty-three seconds each. The identification questions go fast and buy you time for the math. Flag anything that takes more than a minute and move on. And no calculator is allowed, so the math has to be done by hand. Practice it by hand now.

Z: Okay. The math. Let's start with the one everybody gets wrong.

D: Margin versus markup. This is the number one trap in general operations and it will be on your test. Gross profit is selling price minus cost. Simple. The problem is what you divide that profit by. Markup is profit divided by cost. Margin is profit divided by selling price.

Z: Give me numbers.

D: A part costs thirty dollars and sells for sixty. Profit is thirty dollars. Thirty divided by the cost of thirty is one hundred percent. That's markup. Thirty divided by the selling price of sixty is fifty percent. That's margin. Same part, same numbers, two different answers. If the question says gross profit margin and you answer one hundred percent, you just got it wrong.

Z: So how do I remember which is which?

D: Margin is on the money that comes in. Margin, money in, selling price. Markup is what you add to cost. Mark it up from cost. And here's the formula for the other direction. If the question gives you cost and a target margin and asks for selling price, you divide. Cost divided by one minus the margin. Forty-eight dollar cost, forty percent margin. Forty-eight divided by point six is eighty dollars.

Z: And if it gives me markup instead?

D: Then you multiply. Cost times one plus the markup. Forty-eight dollars with a forty percent markup is forty-eight times one point four, which is sixty-seven twenty. Notice that sixty-seven twenty is always one of the wrong answers on a margin question. That's the trap answer. If you see it and it feels right, stop and check which word the question used.

Z: Next, discounts.

D: Two rules. A twenty percent discount means you pay eighty percent. So multiply by point eight. Don't subtract twice, don't get fancy. Second rule, stacked discounts multiply, they never add. Twenty percent off and then ten percent off is not thirty percent off. It's point eight times point nine, which is point seven two. That's a twenty-eight percent total discount.

Z: Example?

D: One hundred fifty dollar item, twenty off then ten off. One fifty times point eight is one twenty. One twenty times point nine is one oh eight. The customer pays one hundred eight dollars. The trap answer is one oh five, which is what you get if you add the discounts to thirty percent.

Z: What about the battery value questions?

D: Those are a P2 staple. Four batteries, four prices, four warranty lengths. Which is the best value? Divide price by months of warranty to get cost per month, and the lowest cost per month wins regardless of the sticker price. Eighty-nine ninety-five with a thirty-six month warranty is two fifty a month. One seventy-nine ninety-five with an eighty-four month warranty is two fourteen a month. The most expensive battery is the best value. The test wants you to ignore sticker shock and do the division.

Z: Decimals and fractions. How much of that do I need?

D: Know the eighths and sixteenths cold. One eighth is point one two five. One quarter is point two five. Three eighths is point three seven five. One half is point five. Five eighths is point six two five. Three quarters is point seven five. And one sixteenth is point zero six two five, so three sixteenths is point one eight seven five and five sixteenths is point three one two five. If they ask you the decimal equivalent of five eighths, the answer is point six two five, and the wrong answers will be things like point five eight and point eight five that look similar.

Z: Conversions?

D: One inch is twenty-five point four millimeters. One quart is point nine four six liters. One gallon is three point seven eight five liters. One foot pound is one point three five six newton meters. One psi is six point eight nine five kilopascals. And a practical one. The store stocks oil in quarts and gallons and a customer needs seven quarts. One gallon is four quarts, so one gallon plus three quarts. Don't overthink it.

Z: Bearing sizes. I've heard those are sneaky.

D: Sneaky because of the decimal place. Engine bearings come in undersizes expressed in thousandths of an inch. Point zero one zero is ten thousandths. Point zero two zero is twenty thousandths. Those are the common ones. Now look at the trap. Point zero zero zero two, point zero zero two, point zero two zero, and point two zero zero are four different parts. If the question says twenty thousandths and you're staring at four part numbers that look alike, twenty thousandths is point zero two zero. Count the places.

Z: Micrometers?

D: Three parts to a reading. The number on the sleeve, plus the lines on the barrel which are each twenty-five thousandths, plus the thimble reading which is in thousandths. So point two seven five on the sleeve plus twelve on the thimble is point two eight seven. A dial caliper reads to a thousandth too.

Z: Let's switch to safety, since that's in general operations.

D: The safety data sheet. Sixteen standard sections, one for every hazardous chemical in the store, and you must provide one to anyone who asks. Flammables go in an approved cabinet. Used oil, used antifreeze, and old batteries go to approved recycling, never down a drain. Battery acid is neutralized with baking soda. And every lifting question has the same answer, lift with your knees, not your back.

Z: And security?

D: The classic fraud patterns. A return with no receipt and a story. A high-dollar order of wheels or electronics on a card where the card isn't present, picked up by a third party. A wholesale phone order from an account you can't verify. If a question describes one of those, that's the one that should raise the flag.

Z: One more from general operations. The purchase order.

D: A purchase order comes from the buyer. It's the customer's written authorization to buy specific quantities at specified prices. Sellers ask for them from commercial accounts. It is not a shipping confirmation, that's a packing slip. It's not a return authorization. Buyer, authorization, purchase. That's a PO.

Z: Let's wrap this one with a quick-fire review. Margin.

D: Profit divided by selling price. Divide to find price.

Z: Markup.

D: Profit divided by cost. Multiply to find price.

Z: Stacked discounts.

D: Multiply the remainders. Twenty and ten is point eight times point nine.

Z: Best battery value.

D: Lowest cost per month of warranty.

Z: Twenty thousandths.

D: Point zero two zero.

Z: Five eighths.

D: Point six two five.

Z: How many questions count?

D: Seventy-five. Answer all eighty-five.

Z: Changing an answer on review.

D: Only with a concrete reason.

Z: That's episode one. Next time, the counter itself. Customers, catalogs, inventory, and the VIN. That's thirty-four percent of the test in one episode, and most of it is common sense if you know the rules.

D: See you there.
